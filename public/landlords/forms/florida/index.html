Notices before court.

3-day notice to pay rent or deliver possession

The statute’s 3-day notice: pay the rent or give back the premises, weekends and legal holidays not counted.

When
Rent is past due (Florida sets no grace period; your lease may).
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.56(3); Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4); Fla. Stat. § 83.56(5)(a)
Good to know
  • The statute’s form is printed word for word. Your name, address and phone number must appear on it (they print as the landlord’s, and under your signature).
  • Florida sets no grace period: serve it any time after rent is late, unless your lease gives the tenant longer.
  • Only court-observed legal holidays are skipped by law; NOHM skips every federal holiday and a few more, so its date is never early.
  • Accepting the full past-due rent, knowing of the default, waives this nonpayment. If you accept partial rent after serving it, you must give a receipt with the date, amount and the new balance due before you file, put the partial rent in the court registry when you file, or serve a new 3-day notice for the new amount (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(5)(a)).
  • No separate demand for possession is needed: if the rent isn’t paid by the date, the rental agreement can be terminated and you may file for possession in county court.
  • Deliver it by mail, by handing the tenant a true copy, by leaving a copy at the residence if the tenant is absent, or by e-mail if you both signed the § 83.505 e-mail addendum (an e-mail is delivered when sent unless it bounces; keep a copy and proof of sending). NOHM adds 5 days when it’s mailed. A lease can’t waive these notices (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4)).

7-day notice to cure

The statute’s 7-day notice: fix the noncompliance, or the lease is terminated.

When
The tenant broke the lease in a way they can fix (pets, guests, parking, cleanliness).
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.56(2)(b); Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4)
Good to know
  • For a noncompliance the tenant can fix: for example unauthorized pets, guests or vehicles, unauthorized parking, or not keeping the premises clean and sanitary (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(2)(b)). Describe it after “You are hereby notified that”.
  • If the same or similar conduct happens again within 12 months, you may file without another notice.
  • Some violations get no chance to cure: intentional destruction, damage or misuse of property, fraudulent entry of a dwelling (from October 1, 2026), or a repeated or continuing unreasonable disturbance, or a similar violation within 12 months of a written warning. Use the 7-day notice to vacate (no cure) for those (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(2)(a)).
  • Accepting rent knowing of the noncompliance waives your right to terminate for it, though not for a continuing one (§ 83.56(5)(a)).
  • Deliver it by mail, by handing the tenant a true copy, by leaving a copy at the residence if the tenant is absent, or by e-mail if you both signed the § 83.505 e-mail addendum (an e-mail is delivered when sent unless it bounces; keep a copy and proof of sending). NOHM adds 5 days when it’s mailed. A lease can’t waive these notices (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4)).

30-day notice to end a month-to-month

Ends a month-to-month at the end of a monthly period, at least 30 days after the tenant gets it.

When
You want a month-to-month tenant out, no fault needed.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.57(3); Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4)
Good to know
  • For a month-to-month: not less than 30 days before the end of a monthly period (Fla. Stat. § 83.57(3)). The other periodic tenancies differ: week to week 7 days before the end of a weekly period, quarter to quarter 30 days, year to year 60 days before the end of the annual period (§ 83.57(1), (2), (4)).
  • A fixed-term lease ends on its date. A lease may require notice of nonrenewal only if it requires it of both of you, between 30 and 60 days (§ 83.575(1)).
  • No separate demand for possession is needed: a tenant who stays after the tenancy ends without your permission is holding over, and you may file for possession and ask for double rent for the holdover (Fla. Stat. § 83.58).
  • Local governments can’t add their own notice rules: the state law preempts them (§ 83.425).
  • Deliver it by mail, by handing the tenant a true copy, by leaving a copy at the residence if the tenant is absent, or by e-mail if you both signed the § 83.505 e-mail addendum (an e-mail is delivered when sent unless it bounces; keep a copy and proof of sending). NOHM adds 5 days when it’s mailed. A lease can’t waive these notices (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4)).

7-day notice of termination (no chance to cure)

The statute’s notice that the lease is terminated now, with 7 days to vacate, for a violation the tenant can’t cure.

When
Intentional damage, fraudulent entry, a repeated unreasonable disturbance, or a repeat within 12 months of a written warning.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.56(2)(a); Fla. Stat. § 83.56(4)
Good to know
  • For noncompliance the tenant shouldn’t be given a chance to cure: destruction, damage or misuse of your or other tenants’ property by intentional act; fraudulent entry of a dwelling unit under § 817.537(2) (from October 1, 2026, whether or not criminal charges were brought); a subsequent or continued unreasonable disturbance; or a similar violation within 12 months of a written warning (Fla. Stat. § 83.56(2)(a)).
  • The words are the statute’s own form. Say exactly what happened after “This action is taken because”.
  • The 7 days run from delivery. NOHM adds 5 days if you mail it.
  • For a violation the tenant can fix, use the 7-day notice to cure instead.
  • Accepting rent knowing of the noncompliance waives your right to terminate for it (§ 83.56(5)(a)).

Court papers.

Eviction filing worksheet (Forms 5, 5a, 6, 7) Court

What to put on the Florida Supreme Court–approved eviction forms, where to get them, and the deadlines after you file.

When
The notice ran out and the tenant hasn’t paid, cured, or left.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.59; Fla. Stat. § 83.60; Fla. Stat. § 83.62; Fla. Stat. § 51.011; Fla. Stat. § 48.183
Good to know
  • File in the county court of the county where the rental is (Fla. Stat. § 83.59(2)), on the Florida Supreme Court–approved forms from The Florida Bar. The Bar warns the forms aren’t guaranteed to track every change in the law: Form 1 still cites the 2007 statute and lacks the 2025 e-mail option.
  • Attach the lease as Exhibit A and the notice you served as Exhibit B.
  • Only you or your lawyer can act after filing: an agent who isn’t a lawyer may file the complaint and nothing more (§ 83.59(2)). A company (an LLC or a corporation) generally needs a Florida lawyer in court (NOHM couldn’t confirm the case law).
  • To collect past-due rent as well as possession, use Form 5a and the damages summons (Form 8); a money judgment needs personal service on the tenant.
  • There is no approved form for a holdover after a tenancy ended by notice or by its term; the complaint describes the unit and the facts that entitle you to it (§§ 83.58, 83.59(2)). Ask a lawyer.

Move-in.

Move-in condition checklist

The written record of the home’s condition when the tenant moves in, signed by both of you.

When
Give it at move-in, and keep the signed copy.
Good to know
  • Florida doesn’t require a move-in checklist. It’s good practice: it is your evidence for a claim on the deposit.
  • At or before the start of the tenancy, give the tenant in writing your name and address, or those of the person authorized to receive notices (Fla. Stat. § 83.50).
  • If you rent 5 or more units: within 30 days of receiving the deposit (or in the lease), tell the tenant where it is held, whether interest is paid, and give the capitalized disclosure Fla. Stat. § 83.49(2) prescribes word for word. NOHM doesn’t print that disclosure: copy it from the statute.
  • For a lease of a year or longer, give the flood disclosure in the form Fla. Stat. § 83.512 prescribes, as a separate document, at or before signing (since October 1, 2025). NOHM doesn’t print it: copy it from the statute.
  • Give the radon statement on a document signed at or before the lease (Fla. Stat. § 404.056(5)): use NOHM’s radon gas disclosure, or put the statement in the lease.
  • Optional statutory papers: the e-mail notice addendum (§ 83.505), the fee-in-lieu-of-deposit disclosures (§ 83.491), the abandoned-property legend in the lease (§ 83.67(5)). A lease that requires notice of nonrenewal must require it of both sides, 30 to 60 days (§ 83.575). The federal lead-paint disclosure applies to homes built before 1978.

Radon gas disclosure

The radon statement Florida requires on a document signed at or before the rental agreement, in the statute’s words.

When
At or before signing the lease.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 404.056(5)
Good to know
  • Florida requires this statement on at least one document signed at or before the rental agreement; it may be in the lease itself (Fla. Stat. § 404.056(5)).
  • Not needed for a transient stay of 45 days or less.
  • Have every adult tenant sign it, and keep it with the lease.

During the lease.

Notice of entry

Tells the tenant when you’ll come in, and why.

When
At least 24 hours before you enter.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.53(2)
Good to know
  • For repairs: at least 24 hours’ notice, and enter between 7:30 a.m. and 8:00 p.m. (Fla. Stat. § 83.53(2)).
  • For other purposes (an inspection, a showing, agreed services) enter with the tenant’s consent, in an emergency, when the tenant unreasonably withholds consent, or when the tenant has been away for half a rental period, unless the rent is current and the tenant told you of the absence (Fla. Stat. § 83.53).
  • Don’t abuse the right of access or use it to harass the tenant.

Notice of rent change

Changes a month-to-month tenant’s rent from a rent due date.

When
At least 30 days before the new rent starts.
Good to know
  • Florida has no statute on notice of a rent increase. During a fixed-term lease the rent changes only as the lease allows. For a month-to-month, NOHM asks for 30 days, the notice that ends a monthly tenancy (Fla. Stat. § 83.57(3)), and starts the new rent on a due date: NOHM’s practice, not a statute.
  • No rent control: local governments can’t regulate tenancies (§ 83.425).
  • You can’t raise rent to retaliate against a tenant’s good-faith complaint (§ 83.64).

Move-out.

Notice of intention to claim on the deposit

The statute’s notice of what you’ll deduct from the deposit, and why, giving the tenant 15 days to object.

When
Within 30 days after the rental agreement ends, if you’ll keep any of the deposit.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 83.49(3)(a)
Good to know
  • Within 30 days after the rental agreement ends, send this by certified mail to the tenant’s last known mailing address, or by e-mail under § 83.505. Miss it and you lose the right to claim against the deposit (Fla. Stat. § 83.49(3)(a)).
  • If you won’t claim anything, don’t send this: return the whole deposit, with any interest owed, within 15 days.
  • The tenant has 15 days after receiving it to object in writing. If they don’t, deduct the claim and send the balance within 30 days of the date of this notice (§ 83.49(3)(b)).
  • Add interest only if the deposit was in an interest-bearing account or under a surety bond (§ 83.49(1)).
  • A tenant who left without 7 days’ written notice, by certified mail or in person, with a forwarding address, relieves you of the notice requirement, but doesn’t forfeit the deposit (§ 83.49(5)).
  • The statute’s form is printed word for word; your address for objections is the one you give as yours.

Notice of right to reclaim abandoned property

The statute’s notice to a former tenant of the property left behind, where to claim it, and by when.

When
After the tenancy ended and the tenant left things behind.
The law
Fla. Stat. § 715.105
Good to know
  • After the tenancy ends and the tenant has left, by eviction or otherwise, send this to the former tenant (and a similar notice to anyone you believe owns any of the property) personally or by first-class mail to the last known address and any other address you know (Fla. Stat. § 715.104).
  • The date to claim it must be at least 10 days after you hand it over, or at least 15 days after you mail it.
  • Store the property with reasonable care, on the premises or in a safe place (§ 715.107). If it’s worth $500 or more and isn’t claimed, it’s sold at a public sale after advertising once a week for two weeks; the balance goes to the county (§ 715.109). Under $500 you may keep, sell or destroy it.
  • If your lease carries the legend of Fla. Stat. § 83.67(5), you need not follow this procedure after the tenant surrenders or abandons the unit, or after the last tenant dies.
  • When the sheriff executes a writ of possession, you may remove property found on the premises to or near the property line instead (§ 83.62(2)).
