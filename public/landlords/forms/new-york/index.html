Notices before court.

14-day rent demand

Demands the rent owed, or the home back, within 14 days, with the Good Cause Eviction Law notice attached.

When
Rent is unpaid; if it’s 5 days late, send the late-rent notice by certified mail first.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 711(2); N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 735(1); N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 702(1); N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 231-c(1); N.Y. Gen. Constr. Law § 25-a(1)
Good to know
  • First, if rent isn’t received within 5 days of the due date, send the tenant a written notice of nonpayment by certified mail (Real Prop. Law § 235-e(d)); NOHM has it as the 5-day late-rent notice. Without it, the tenant has a defense to the case.
  • You can’t serve it yourself: someone 18 or older who isn’t a party to the case must (UCS Landlord’s Guide). Leave it with someone, or post it, only after a reasonable try at handing it over; the courts’ guide reads that as two tries at different times.
  • Attach the Good Cause Eviction Law notice, filled in to say whether the premises are covered and, if exempt, why (Real Prop. Law § 231-c). Get it from the courts’ Landlord’s Guide or New York State Homes and Community Renewal.
  • When service by leaving it with someone or posting it is complete isn’t settled for a rent demand; NOHM adds 3 days when you serve it that way.
  • If the tenant pays all the rent due before the hearing, you must accept it and the case ends (RPAPL § 731(4)).
  • Late fees: only after rent is 5 days late, and at most $50 or 5% of the monthly rent, whichever is less (Real Prop. Law § 238-a(2)). They can’t go in this demand.
  • Where the Good Cause Eviction Law applies, unpaid rent is good cause only if it didn’t come from an unreasonable rent increase (Real Prop. Law § 216(1)(a)).
  • New York City’s Housing Court, and rent-stabilized units, have rules of their own; use a lawyer there.

Notice to cure a lease violation

Tells the tenant what to stop or fix, and by when; New York leaves the period to the lease.

When
The tenant broke a lease term (10 days to cure where the Good Cause Eviction Law applies).
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 216(1)(b); N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 753(4)

The statute doesn’t set this notice’s exact words: check it before you serve one.

Good to know
  • Follow your lease: its notice to cure and its notice ending the tenancy, in its periods. NOHM’s 30 days is longer than most leases ask.
  • Where the Good Cause Eviction Law applies (New York City and the localities that opted in), the tenant must have failed to stop after written notice that the violation cease within 10 days of receipt (Real Prop. Law § 216(1)(b)).
  • After a judgment for a lease breach, the court must stay the warrant 30 days so the tenant can fix it (RPAPL § 753(4)).
  • Serve it as a rent demand is served (RPAPL § 735), the safest way.

30, 60 or 90-day notice of non-renewal

Tells the tenant you won’t renew, or ends a month-to-month, with the notice the time they’ve lived there calls for.

When
You won’t renew the lease, or want to end a month-to-month tenancy.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 226-c(1)(a); N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 226-c(2); N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 232-a; N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 231-c(1)
Good to know
  • Real Prop. Law § 226-c(2) sets the notice by the longer of how long the tenant has lived there and the lease’s term: under a year, 30 days; one to two years, 60; two years or more, 90. NOHM counts from the lease and ends the tenancy on the last day of a rental period, to be safe.
  • Given late, the tenancy goes on under its existing terms until the notice period has run from the day you gave it (§ 226-c(1)(a)).
  • Where the Good Cause Eviction Law applies (New York City and the cities, towns and villages that opted in), a covered tenancy can end only for a reason in Real Prop. Law § 216; say which on the attached notice. Check whether your locality opted in.
  • In New York City, the notice must be served the way a notice of petition is (Real Prop. Law § 232-a); NOHM’s ways of serving are those (RPAPL § 735), and NOHM adds 3 days when you leave it with someone or post it.
  • A fixed-term lease ends on its date with no notice to terminate, but this non-renewal notice is still required (§ 226-c).
  • Taking rent after the date can create a new tenancy, and a judge may dismiss your case (UCS Landlord’s Guide).
  • New York requires no separate demand for possession: if the tenant stays after the date, you may bring a holdover proceeding (RPAPL § 711(1)).

5-day late-rent notice (certified mail)

The written notice the law requires when rent isn’t received within 5 days of its due date, sent by certified mail.

When
Rent wasn’t received within 5 days of the due date: send it before the 14-day rent demand.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 235-e(d)
Good to know
  • Send it by certified mail, and keep a copy and the certified-mail receipt: the courts’ nonpayment petition asks you to attach both.
  • Without it, the tenant can use the missing notice as an affirmative defense to a nonpayment case (Real Prop. Law § 235-e(d)).
  • It starts no period. Serve the 14-day rent demand next.

Court papers.

Eviction filing worksheet (UCS-LTN1, UCS-LTN2, UCS-LTH1, UCS-LTH2)

The courts’ notice of petition and petition for your case, where to get them, the values from your lease to copy onto them, and the steps and deadlines.

When
The rent demand ran out, or the tenancy ended, and the tenant hasn’t left.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 711; N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 731; N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 733(1); N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 735; N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 741; N.Y. Real Prop. Acts. Law § 749
Good to know
  • The courts publish their own notice of petition and petition for eviction cases outside New York City; the courts’ forms page says they’re required in City, District, Town and Village Courts from September 3, 2024 (NOHM saw that in a search result; the page wouldn’t open). NOHM fills in this worksheet for you to copy from, not a petition of its own. The worksheet isn’t filed.
  • A company landlord: in City and District Court, a corporation or voluntary association must appear with a lawyer; in Town or Village Court, a corporation may appear with a lawyer or an authorized officer, director or employee (UCS Landlord’s Guides). The guides don’t name LLCs: NOHM couldn’t confirm their rule, so ask the clerk or a lawyer.
  • New York City’s Housing Court has its own forms and rules, and many units there are rent-stabilized: use a lawyer.
  • Newburgh, Syracuse and Schenectady also ask the petition to show the rental is registered (RPAPL § 741(7)–(9)); NOHM read those items only in summary.
  • The courts’ do-it-yourself forms program isn’t open to corporations, owners of 3 or more units, or New York City property.
  • Bring the lease, the 5-day notice and its certified-mail receipt, the 14-day demand or the termination notice with the affidavit of service, the Good Cause notice, and the rent ledger.

Move-in.

Move-in condition agreement

The written record of the home’s condition when the tenant moves in, signed by both of you.

When
After the lease is signed, before move-in, if the tenant asks for the inspection.
The law
N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(c)
Good to know
  • After the lease is signed and before the tenant moves in, offer to inspect the home together. If the tenant asks for the inspection, you both sign this agreement before they move in (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(c)).
  • You can’t keep any of the deposit for a condition noted here.
  • Every lease and renewal carries the Good Cause Eviction Law notice (Real Prop. Law § 231-c(1)), the flood disclosure (§ 231-b) and the sprinkler notice (§ 231-a): NOHM has the flood and sprinkler forms.
  • When you deposit the security in a bank, tell the tenant in writing where (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-103(2)): NOHM has that notice. The deposit can’t exceed one month’s rent (§ 7-108(1-a)(a)).
  • Give a written receipt for rent paid in cash or by anything but the tenant’s own check (Real Prop. Law § 235-e). Application fees: only the actual cost of a background or credit check, at most $20 (§ 238-a(1)).
  • Homes built before 1978: the federal lead-based paint disclosure. New York City adds its own (bedbug history, lead, window guards); NOHM hasn’t confirmed those.

Flood history and risk disclosure

The four flood questions every New York residential lease must answer, and the statute’s notice about flood insurance.

When
With every residential lease: attach it when the lease is signed or renewed.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 231-b
Good to know
  • Every residential lease must disclose these four things and carry the flood insurance notice (Real Prop. Law § 231-b).
  • Check the premises against FEMA’s current Flood Insurance Rate Maps. Disclose any prior flood damage you know of, or reasonably should know of, and what it was.
  • Attach it to the lease, and have every tenant sign.

Sprinkler system notice

The bold-face notice every New York residential lease must give of whether the premises have a working sprinkler system.

When
With every residential lease: attach it when the lease is signed or renewed.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 231-a
Good to know
  • Every residential lease must give conspicuous notice, in bold face type, of whether the leased premises have a maintained and operative sprinkler system, and if they do, the last date it was maintained and inspected (Real Prop. Law § 231-a).

Notice of where the deposit is held

Tells the tenant, in writing, the bank where you deposited their security and how much.

When
When you deposit the security in a bank.
The law
N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-103(2)
Good to know
  • The deposit stays the tenant’s money, held in trust, never mixed with yours (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-103(1)).
  • When you put it in a bank, tell each tenant who paid it, in writing, the bank’s name and address and the amount (§ 7-103(2)). The bank must have a place of business in New York.
  • A building with 6 or more units: the deposit must be in an interest-bearing account at the prevailing rate (§ 7-103(2-a)). In an interest-bearing account, you may keep one percent a year on the deposit for administration.

During the lease.

Notice of entry

Tells the tenant when you’ll come in, and why.

When
Before you enter for an inspection, a repair or a showing.
Good to know
  • No New York statute sets notice for entry. NOHM suggests at least 24 hours; your lease may say more. The Attorney General’s tenants’ guide advises reasonable notice (NOHM didn’t re-read it).
  • New York City’s housing rules set their own notice for inspections and repairs; NOHM hasn’t confirmed them.
  • Where the Good Cause Eviction Law applies, a tenant who unreasonably refuses access for repairs, or to show the unit to a buyer or lender, gives you good cause (Real Prop. Law § 216(1)(f)).
  • A move-out inspection the tenant asked for needs at least 48 hours’ written notice (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(d)).

Notice of rent change

Tells the tenant the higher rent you’ll renew at, with the 30, 60 or 90 days’ notice the law sets.

When
At least 30, 60 or 90 days before the new rent starts, by how long the tenant has lived there or the lease’s term.
The law
N.Y. Real Prop. Law § 226-c
Good to know
  • An offer to renew with a rent increase of 5% or more needs written notice: 30 days if the tenant has lived there under a year and has a lease under a year; 60 days for one to two years, or a lease of one to two years; 90 days for two years or more, or a lease of two years or more (Real Prop. Law § 226-c(2)). NOHM uses it for any increase.
  • Given late, the tenancy goes on under its existing terms from the day you gave notice until the notice period runs (§ 226-c(1)(a)).
  • Attach the Good Cause Eviction Law notice (§ 231-c(1)). In a covered unit, an increase above the local rent standard (the lower of 5% plus the regional consumer price index change, or 10%) needs a justification on it, and unpaid rent from an unreasonable increase isn’t good cause to evict (§ 216(1)(a)).
  • New York has no statewide rent cap. Rent-stabilized and rent-controlled units have their own rules.
  • During a lease, change the rent only as the lease allows.

Move-out.

Notice of the right to a move-out inspection

Tells the tenant they may ask for an inspection before moving out, and be there for it.

When
Soon after you or the tenant gives notice to end the tenancy.
The law
N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(d)
Good to know
  • Within a reasonable time after either of you gives notice to end the tenancy, tell the tenant in writing of their right to ask for an inspection before moving out, and to be there (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(d)). Not needed if the tenant gave less than two weeks’ notice.
  • If they ask, inspect no earlier than two weeks and no later than one week before the end, on at least 48 hours’ written notice; then give them an itemized statement of the repairs or cleaning you’d deduct for, and let them fix it before they leave.

Security deposit: itemized deductions

The written list of what you kept from the deposit, and why, with the refund.

When
Within 14 days after the tenant moves out.
The law
N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(e)
Good to know
  • Within 14 days after the tenant moves out, give an itemized statement of the basis for anything you keep, and return the rest. Miss it and you forfeit any right to keep any of the deposit (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(e)).
  • You must prove what you keep is reasonable (§ 7-108(1-a)(f)). A willful violation can cost punitive damages up to twice the deposit (§ 7-108(1-a)(g)).
  • Nothing for ordinary wear and tear, a prior tenant’s damage, or a condition noted in the move-in agreement.
  • Interest: a deposit in an interest-bearing account earns it for the tenant, less the 1% a year you may keep (§ 7-103(2)). Rent-stabilized units have their own rules.

Notice of property left behind

Lists what the tenant left, and how long you’ll hold it before you dispose of it.

When
After the tenancy ended and the tenant left things behind.
The law
N.Y. Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(b)
Good to know
  • No New York statute sets a notice, a storage period or a sale for a tenant’s belongings. NOHM fills in 30 days to hold them; ask a lawyer before you dispose of anything of value.
  • Use it only after the tenant has moved out, or the officer executed the warrant: never while they still have possession. When an officer executes a warrant, the officer removes the tenant’s possessions (UCS Landlord’s Guide).
  • You may charge the reasonable, itemized cost of moving and storing them to the deposit (Gen. Oblig. Law § 7-108(1-a)(b)).
